Statement fetcher

Logs into my banks, downloads new statements, and files them into a document archive that already had opinions. It worked on day one. Everything worth writing down happened after that.

Source on GitHub → · Python · Playwright · 110 offline tests

Why it exists

Four banks, six accounts, once a month: log in, click through to statements, download a PDF, rename it, drag it into the right folder. Twenty minutes a computer should obviously be doing, and the kind of chore that quietly stops happening until the year you need it to have happened.

The archive has rules. A statement goes in Finance/Assets/Banking/<Bank>/<Account>/<year>/ as 2026-09-24_BofA_Statement_x3984.pdf, and the year in that path is the year the statement period ended — not the year I got around to downloading it.

The second run is the real design problem

Downloading a statement is easy. Downloading it twice is the bug. Two checks run, cheapest first: 

listed on the page  →  already on disk?  →  yes: never download it
                            │
                            no
                            ▼
                    download  →  hash  →  in the ledger?  →  yes: skip

The first check is what makes a monthly run cost one page load per account instead of re-pulling a year of PDFs to hash them. The second is the one that still works after you have moved, renamed or deleted a file by hand: every filed statement is recorded by the SHA-256 of its bytes in an append-only ledger, and the ledger is the source of truth, not the folder.

Look at the destination before you write to it

The code generated BankofAmerica. Ninety-eight files already on disk said BofA, and credit cards said CC-Statement where everything else said Statement. Nothing would have crashed. It would have written thirty-five near-duplicates beside statements that were already there, and I would have noticed in a year when the folders had two names for every document.

So the institution has two names on purpose: the folder says "Bank of America", the files say "BofA", and conflating them is a config error rather than an assumption.

The structure was wrong, and only the archive could tell me

Four accounts at one bank filed into Bank of America/2026/. On paper that is fine — every filename carries the last four, nothing collides, every test passed. Then I opened the folder: September had four statements in it, from two checking accounts and two credit cards, and finding the one I wanted meant reading a masked number off each filename. The tool had faithfully automated a layout that only worked when I was filing by hand.

Each account got its own folder — Savings 8256, name and number together, because two cards at one bank can share a product name but never a last four. The change was one line. What it dragged along was not:

  • The duplicate check has to straddle both layouts. Point it at the new path and every statement on disk becomes invisible; the next run re-downloads a year of history and files a second copy of all of it. Lookups search the account folder and the old flat one.
  • Migrating is its own command. statements reorganize is dry by default, prints every move, and writes a flushed undo log before it touches anything. A hundred and sixty-one files through a personal archive is not something to do without a way back.
  • Some files cannot be placed. Twenty-two had no account number anywhere in the name. They are listed and left exactly where they are, because a wrong guess buries a document somewhere I will never look again.

Where the automation stops

When a bank asks for a verification code, the tool stops and hands over the open browser. It does not read codes from mail, from messages, or from anywhere else. For anything touching money the second factor is a good place to stop — and credentials live in the macOS Keychain rather than a file that every backup daemon on the machine would copy.

A monitor that can only report good news reports nothing

The monthly run texts a summary over iMessage. It took three separate silent failures to get there — an unset environment variable that made the notifier return quietly, a launchd job that does not inherit your shell, and a run that found zero statements and said nothing at all. The last one is the important one: an empty month and a broken scraper look identical unless the tool says which it was.

Shape

statements login "Wells Fargo"   once per bank, into the Keychain
statements doctor                check every login, download nothing
statements fetch --dry-run       say what would happen
statements reorganize            print every move, change nothing
statements install               a launchd job, monthly

110 tests, none of which touch the network, over the parts that must not be wrong: where a file goes, what it is called, and whether it has been filed before.
